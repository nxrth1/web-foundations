# Week 8 Reflection

The hardest concept for me was turning traffic estimates into design decisions, especially understanding that a daily or 10-minute average does not describe a sudden burst. I worked through it by writing down the assumptions, converting totals into per-second rates, and then tracing what happens when many users try to claim the same resource at once. The TicketHub seat-hold example helped connect database transactions and constraints to a real correctness problem.

The feedback showed me that a sound high-level design is not enough if the critical correctness path is left implicit. I completed the seats schema with constraints that reject inconsistent inventory states, and clarified how a transaction locks requested seats, checks availability, and rolls back the entire hold if any seat cannot be claimed. That makes the outcome of two buyers requesting the same seat concrete: one transaction can commit the hold, while the other must fail after it sees the committed state. The architecture diagram and trade-offs are also included so the database strategy is connected to the rest of the system.

Next, I want to learn more about CDNs: how caching rules, expiration, and invalidation work, and how to decide which content is safe to cache. I would like to apply that to a small project and measure whether it improves load time without serving stale information. For TicketHub, I would keep cached seat maps advisory and measure cache freshness separately from the authoritative hold path.
